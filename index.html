Introduction

Design engineer · Educator · Systems thinker

Bill Mead creates technology in service of specific people and practical needs.

Bill was my professor for Interactive Design and User Experience/User Interface Design at the University of California, Davis. He is a design engineer and educator whose work brings together software development, visual design, systems thinking, and pedagogy. He teaches design and web-development courses at UC Davis and community colleges and previously served as Director of Design at the Art Institute. Throughout his career, Bill has created specialized software for specific communities and practical needs, including a database-driven system that helped faculty generate thousands of accurate course syllabi.

His approach to design engineering is grounded in craft, technical understanding, intellectual curiosity, and service. As both a practitioner and educator, he continually researches emerging tools and considers how artificial intelligence is changing what designers need to learn, how they develop judgment, and what responsible leadership looks like in a rapidly evolving field.

The conversation

A senior-level perspective on design engineering, craft, judgment, and leadership that Bill has extended into his pedagogical pursuits. Each answer preserves his examples, analogies, qualifications, and uncertainty.

Bill Mead

It really is the question of craft and understanding how much time you need to invest in learning a craft that can now be performed, at least partially, by a machine. That is the thing I am really not sure about. I just do not know yet.

When I use AI to build things, I notice that there are times when it feels really great. I might think, “Holy shit, this figured out something that I could have figured out on my own, but it would have taken me all day, and it did it in fifteen minutes.” If I understand what it did and can see how it solved the problem, that can feel really useful and exciting.

Then there are other times when it feels really yucky. For example, I used Midjourney to generate some visual images because I was being lazy and did not want to take the time to design them myself. I knew the images would have been much better if I had designed them myself, and using AI that way did not feel good.

Trying to navigate which uses of this technology feel productive and which feel wrong is a constant battle. Right now, all we can really do is monitor ourselves. It is probably going to be years before there are meaningful guidelines for this technology.

I was also asked to judge the UC Davis hackathon this spring. It was my second year doing it, and nearly everything I saw was AI-generated. I think I saw one project that considered design in an interesting way. I am not saying the students did not use AI, because they probably did, but at least they appeared to be considering design, users, and actual needs.

With most of the other projects, AI was built into the product, the visuals were created by AI, and the code was created by AI. It was AI from beginning to end. The projects looked more polished than they had the previous year because AI could produce so much for the students, but you really had to dig to find good ideas underneath that polish. That is the concern. A project can look finished without showing much evidence of design thinking, creativity, or craft.

Bill Mead

I wonder about that too. I do not really know, and I think it is still too early to have a definitive answer.

My sense is that if you really want to do this work, then yes, you need to understand how to architect software. You need to know how to make decisions. You need to know how to get AI to code something the way you want it coded, how to make it follow your style guide, and how you want the code organized. There is a lot of higher-level knowledge involved, even if you are no longer writing every line manually.

I was talking to Eric Howard, who owns Direct Message in Sacramento. He is a software engineer, designer, and artist. He used an analogy about pottery. If you are going to make pottery, do you need to go out and dig the clay yourself, or can you go to the store and buy the clay?

I understand that argument. You do not necessarily need to perform every preliminary task yourself. On the other hand, when we are teaching students, I think they still need to dig some of the clay to understand clay. There is a certain amount of the process that you have to experience yourself. The difficult question is how much.

There is a connection between learning a craft, practicing it, and putting in the hard effort. I watched a video about a band discussing its most famous song. One of the musicians explained that the beginning of the song originated as a finger exercise he used to warm up on the guitar. The rest of the band heard it and thought it sounded interesting, and it eventually became the opening of their most famous song.

That idea came out of practice. If you are not doing those exercises and engaging directly with the material, how do you develop the taste and judgment necessary to recognize what is interesting? How do you know what to ask an AI system to make? There is a connection between technical practice and judgment, although I do not yet know exactly where that connection lies or how much foundational practice a person needs.

Bill Mead

Understanding how software is made at a very fundamental level is extremely important. How do you divide the files? How do you architect an application? What strategies do you use to build the individual pieces and put them together?

From an engineering perspective, that is the knowledge that AI is likely to handle at only a basic or average level. It will often do the bare minimum. It will move toward the middle or the mean. The result will be “meh” unless somebody with more knowledge provides direction and recognizes where it is going wrong.

I was recently talking to Matthew Kwan, another graduate of the design department. He said he lets Claude Code do everything, but he cannot read the code because it generates everything in React. I will not let it do that. I tell it I want plain vanilla JavaScript because I want to understand what it is doing and be able to figure out where it has gone wrong.

In a project I am working on now, I was asking the tool to make both functional and stylistic changes. It was very good at the functional changes. The functionality worked, and I did not have to spend much time looking at that part of the code. The styling changes, however, were much more difficult for it. It did not get them right at least half of the time, and this was just CSS. I had to go into the code and correct the CSS myself.

In another project, I only wanted to center an element on the screen. All it needed was a width and margin: auto. That was essentially two lines of code, but the AI was doing backflips trying to center this one element. Because I understood CSS, I could recognize that it was overcomplicating a simple problem and fix it myself.

The technology is improving, and it is considerably better than it was a year ago. Even so, it still does not consistently make good visual and stylistic decisions. Design engineers need enough technical knowledge to understand the output, diagnose problems, and correct the work when the tool does something unnecessary or inappropriate.

Bill Mead

I recently watched a video from a senior UX/UI designer who had been doing this work for about fifteen years. She was considering leaving her position because her role had changed so dramatically.

For years, the product managers would outline the goals of a project. The UX/UI designers would develop the specifications for how the product should look and behave, and then the work would go to the developers to build. Now, product managers can quickly generate prototypes themselves.

The problem is that the prototypes are often not very good. They may use the branding incorrectly or fail to follow the organization’s design system because the product managers have never needed to know those things. They have not previously been responsible for that part of the process.

This designer said her role had become more like a consultant. A product manager would generate the beginning of a prototype, and then she would have to explain that it did not follow the brand guidelines or the established design system. She described herself as becoming the “brand police.”

She was frustrated because she did not get to design anything anymore. Other people were producing the initial work, but they were not doing it very well. She felt as if she were constantly slowing down the process because the product managers could generate something very quickly, while she had to say, “Hold your horses. This needs to be fixed.” She would ultimately have to rebuild much of what they had made. That was not especially creative or enjoyable, but it was necessary.

I thought her perspective showed both sides of the problem. These tools allow people to iterate and create faster, but the ability to create something quickly does not mean they know what to create. Having the discipline and knowledge to determine what should be made is probably still the most important part.

I think the work of designers may become less focused on the individual artifact and what it looks like. It may become more focused on designing the system, developing the design strategy, and defining a design system that other people or tools can implement.

More than ever, designers are going to be needed. If you watch people who promote AI tools, they often demonstrate them by creating Tetris, Snake, a Twitter clone, or something else that already exists in thousands of forms. AI can reproduce those familiar patterns because it has many examples.

Designers, however, solve unique problems created by particular business propositions, users, and circumstances. Those situations involve questions that AI does not already have answers to. There are still problems that need to be identified, framed, understood, and solved. Designers will be needed for those things.

Bill Mead

I think that is a very valid concern, and I do think there will be a trend in that direction.

The analogy I like to use is furniture. If you think about furniture made a hundred years ago, such as Shaker furniture, there was a highly developed craft involved in making it. Today, 90 percent of us will go to IKEA or Target and buy a table. It is not necessarily a beautifully crafted table, but it serves its purpose.

However, if you are Apple and you are developing an Apple Store, you are probably not furnishing it with tables from IKEA. Someone is designing and fabricating those objects specifically for that environment.

We are likely going to make and use much more software, and a large percentage of it will not be well crafted. That does not mean there will no longer be a need for well-crafted software.

My wife is currently volunteering to photograph garments for a weaving competition. We were discussing the garments at dinner, and she described how beautiful they were. Before machines automated the production of inexpensive clothing, weaving and garment-making were highly developed crafts. People became extraordinarily skilled and fast at making those things. Then the machines came along and completely transformed the process.

It struck me that this is a metaphor for exactly what is happening with software. There is a craft on the coding side of software, although that craft can be difficult to communicate because users ideally never see the code. They do not know whether the underlying code is well crafted.

In terms of design, however, there is a recognizable difference between a Porsche and a Toyota RAV4 in the quality of the design and craftsmanship. There will always be an audience that wants a crafted experience.

At the same time, almost all the software I have made has been for a very niche audience. That type of software is going to explode because people can now create unusual or highly personalized products that might not be intended for anyone but themselves. They can potentially make those products in an afternoon, and that is exciting.

If you are a small business, you may not need an enormous Salesforce package with millions of features. If you only need a system for creating invoices, you might be able to make software that performs exactly that function without paying for a massive enterprise product. There may be many opportunities for designers in that space because people will not necessarily know how to begin defining or designing those products, even when the software itself becomes easier to build.

Bill Mead

I think the real answer is wisdom.

We can think of AI as a genie in a bottle. You rub the lamp, the genie comes out, and you ask it to grant your wishes. We have versions of those stories across many cultures, and how often do the wishes go wrong?

When I encountered those stories as a kid, I always thought that if I had a genie, I would ask good questions. I would ask for the right things. I would not fall into the trap of asking for something that would create terrible consequences.

As an adult, I think the chances that I would actually be the one person capable of asking for exactly the right things are probably quite low.

We now live in an age when information, or “intelligence” in quotation marks, is abundant and inexpensive. What we need is the wisdom to determine what we should do with it. What should we ask from the genie in the bottle? What should we choose not to ask for? What will the consequences be?

That is what I wish designers had, or what I hope they will develop: the wisdom to determine how we should use these technologies.

Bill Mead

Come up with a passion project, an application, or something else you find genuinely interesting. See whether you can build it and make it look and function the way you want it to look and function. Use some of these tools to help with the coding and see how far you can get.

At the very least, the design side of design engineering has a lot of opportunity. The fact that you may not need to know as much code as you once did could be an advantage. You can take the pieces you do know and use them to build something.

I still do not know exactly how much technical foundation a person needs, but you should know enough to understand what the tool is doing and identify where it is going wrong. That is why I ask AI to produce plain JavaScript rather than code I cannot understand. I want to retain the ability to evaluate and modify the work.

Your music-transposition application and ASL-learning application are great examples of projects to continue developing. They are based on specific interests and genuine needs. Mapping sign language through a camera involves difficult visual and mathematical problems. Even if the prototype is imperfect, it shows that you are engaging with something challenging.

Hiring managers are probably tired of seeing the same grocery-list applications and redesigned Spotify interfaces. Those projects can still teach useful skills, but personal projects based on unusual interests can help someone distinguish themselves. They also give the designer a reason to remain invested in solving the problem.

It is also important to seek companies and opportunities with values that align with your own values around design, purpose, and what the work is ultimately for. Those opportunities exist. They may not be easy to find, and it may take time, but there are people who care about doing thoughtful work rather than simply producing things and making money.

Bill Mead

Almost every software project I have created has been for a specific niche group and designed to solve a specific niche problem. That has always been gratifying, especially when I can see the product being used or use it myself.

One particular example comes from my time at the Art Institute, when I was the Director of Design. I built a tool that allowed faculty to generate syllabi for their courses.

An accrediting body had visited the school and discovered that the course descriptions included in faculty syllabi did not always match the official descriptions in the course catalog. Until then, the school created syllabi by passing Microsoft Word files around. An instructor might read the course description in the document, decide they did not like it, and rewrite it. The description would then no longer match the official catalog.

A course syllabus is composed of approximately three types of content. First, there is information that applies to every course, such as accessibility policies and policies for students with disabilities. Second, there is information that applies to a specific course, including its official description and student learning outcomes. Third, there is information created by the instructor, such as the weekly schedule, meeting times, assignments, and contact information.

I created a database-driven system that pulled in the institution-wide policies and official course information. Instructors only had to enter the information specific to themselves and the way they planned to teach the course. The system provided a simple web form and generated a completed syllabus as a PDF.

Faculty loved it because they could produce their syllabi in minutes. The administration loved it because a staff member no longer had to inspect every syllabus and compare every student learning outcome with the official catalog.

The dean told me I should sell the system, but I did not think there was much of a market for it. Schools are not necessarily inclined to invest money simply to save time for faculty and administrators when they cannot identify an immediate financial return. Even so, it was incredibly satisfying to make something that solved a real problem. Before the school closed, the system had generated somewhere between 5,000 and 8,000 syllabi.

That is the kind of work I find meaningful. It does not have to be glamorous. It should be useful to the people for whom it was designed.

Bill Mead

I look for leaders who understand that the organizational chart in most organizations is upside down.

If you look at the organizational chart for UC Davis, it will place the president at the top and the students at the bottom. That structure should be reversed. The students should be at the top. The people directly beneath them should be those who work with and support the students. The president should be at the bottom.

Good leaders understand that they are there to support their people. It is not that everyone else works for the leader. The leader works for their people.

Leaders should keep unnecessary problems off their people’s desks so those people can do the best job possible. They should remove obstacles, secure the support their teams need, and advocate for the people doing the work.

Leaders who put themselves on a pedestal, assume everyone is there because they report to them, and simply boss people around do not understand leadership.

In an educational setting, for example, a strong department chair should fight for the students and the program. The chair should press the administration for the money, staffing, and resources the department needs to succeed. If enrollment grows, leadership should recognize that the program needs additional support rather than treating its growth only as a problem.

Leadership requires understanding whose needs the organization exists to serve. It also requires being willing to challenge higher levels of the organization when their priorities interfere with the people and purpose of the program.

Bill Mead

I recently saw an excellent interview with the CEO of Patreon. He is a musician who has been involved with bands, and he started Patreon as a way for people to connect with audiences and build communities around their creative work.

He discussed the decisions Patreon has made that run against the common approach to social media. Much of social media is designed primarily to capture attention. Patreon is trying to build something that is not simply about attention. It was refreshing to see someone working with a team to make something they genuinely believe in rather than focusing strictly on enriching themselves.

I also listened to an interview with the CEO of GoFundMe. The only money GoFundMe makes from many campaigns comes from optional tips people leave for the platform. The money collected for a person asking for help goes to that person unless the donor separately chooses to tip GoFundMe. Their stated purpose is to give people opportunities to help one another.

Those examples interest me because they show leaders making choices based on what they believe the platform should do and whom it should serve.

For designers, it is important to look for companies and opportunities that share their values around design and its purpose. There is a broad spectrum of work in the industry. At one end, people use AI to automate the production of enormous quantities of meaningless content. At the other, people are using technology to solve specific problems and create things they believe are worthwhile.

The work that aligns with your values is out there. I am not saying it will be easy to find, but I think it exists. Leadership involves establishing a meaningful purpose and helping a team make decisions that remain aligned with it.

04

Reflection

What I learned

Role, challenge, leadership

01 · The role

My conversation with Bill helped me understand that design engineering is not defined simply by the ability to design an interface and then write the code needed to build it. The role involves understanding how software is structured, recognizing how technical decisions affect the user experience, and moving between conceptual thinking and implementation. A design engineer must be able to define a problem, determine how a system should work, and evaluate whether the result meets the needs of the people it was intended to serve. Bill’s syllabus-generation system demonstrates this broader understanding of the role. Its value did not come from novelty or visual spectacle. It came from recognizing a systemic problem, separating the information into appropriate categories, designing a technical structure around those categories, and making the process easier for both faculty and administrators. This showed me that design engineering can be highly practical, specialized, and service-oriented.

02 · The challenge

I also learned that one of the role’s central challenges is preserving craft and judgment when technology makes production faster and easier. Bill did not offer a simple rejection or endorsement of AI. Instead, he described an unresolved tension between using AI to overcome technical barriers and allowing it to replace the practice through which designers develop understanding, taste, and creative judgment. His analogy about digging clay was particularly meaningful to me. A designer may not need to complete every stage of production manually, but they need enough direct experience with the material to understand what they are making and recognize when something is wrong. AI can produce functional code, polished images, and convincing prototypes, but those outputs may conceal generic thinking, inappropriate design decisions, or poorly structured systems. Design engineers must therefore develop enough technical fluency to interrogate the output, enough visual and interaction knowledge to refine it, and enough wisdom to decide when automation is serving the work rather than hollowing it out.

03 · Leadership from within

Finally, I learned that leadership from within design engineering is less about authority than responsibility and service. Bill’s description of the inverted organizational chart reframed leadership for me. The people an organization exists to serve should be placed at the top, followed by those who work with them directly. Formal leaders should see themselves as supporting that work from below. Within a design-engineering team, this means removing barriers, advocating for resources, protecting the conditions required for thoughtful work, and keeping the team aligned with the needs of users. It also means questioning whether a product’s goals are socially meaningful or primarily extractive. Bill’s examples suggest that technical leadership cannot be separated from values. A design-engineering leader determines not only how a system will be built, but also what it is for, whose needs it prioritizes, and whether the team’s capabilities are being directed toward something genuinely worth making.
